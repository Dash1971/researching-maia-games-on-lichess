02 / Two crowds, with an overlap

A bigger casual audience.
A familiar pattern of regulars.

We see 128,628 casual accounts and 93,020 rated accounts. Only 11.9% of the 198,106 distinct observed handles appear in both formats.

Many visitors. A very active core.

In both formats, roughly four in ten accounts appear just once. Yet just 1% of accounts contribute more than half the games. Casual play has the larger observed audience; neither format is evenly spread across its accounts.

04 / Choose the position. Play it out.

A possible practice room
inside casual chess.

5.8%

of casual games are tagged
from position.

118,056 games began with the fromPosition tag, versus 1,922,672 tagged standard. That is roughly one casual game in seventeen.

The same records are 3.4% of all casual and rated games combined. None of the collected rated games have this tag.

What might people be practicing?

Our interpretation: choosing a starting position makes Maia a plausible partner for focused practice. These are possible uses, not measured categories:

01

Rehearse an opening

Start at a familiar branching point and play out the plans that follow, without repeating the first few moves every time.

02

Replay a turning point

Return to a middlegame position from an earlier game, try a different idea, and see how the continuation feels.

03

Finish the endgame

Set up a rook ending or a winning position, then practice converting the advantage or holding a difficult defense.

A practice possibility, not proof of practice. The tag tells us how the game was set up; it does not tell us the position, the goal, or whether an account improved. Some games could be experiments, unusual setups or automated activity. One account, top1mostplayedgames, contributes 23.3% of all from-position games, so the volume does not imply equally widespread use.

Exact starting positions and moves were not saved. We cannot separate openings, middlegames and endings, identify repeated positions, or establish a training benefit. For an illustration of choosing a position, see the Lichess board editor and its “Continue from here” options.

07 / More games does not always mean more people

Both grew.
Rated grew faster.

+79.4%rated games
+37.7%casual games
+24.4%casual, excluding one account’s September games

Earliest complete 12 months: Nov 2023–Oct 2024. Latest: Oct 2025–Sep 2026. This is not a comparison of consecutive years. Partial October 2023 and October 2026 are excluded. Across these blocks, casual’s share of combined games falls from 57.8% to 51.2%.

One account, 63,504 September games. That is 55.6% of the month’s casual volume. Excluding only those games leaves 50,729 casual September games, versus 53,754 in August. Meanwhile, casual active accounts went from 4,594 in August to 4,446 in September. The volume spike is not a surge in account numbers.

The sensitivity view retains that account’s other months and all other games. It is a concentration check, not a claim about its identity, motives or legitimacy. We have not verified that every opposing account is human.

08 / What this comparison can tell us

Shared habits.
Different contexts.

Similarities

The same most-played Maia, the same top three clocks, many one-game visitors and a small core producing most of the games. Those patterns recur in both formats.

Differences

A larger casual audience, limited observed overlap, somewhat more slow chess, and different results—even within a selected matched group. The raw format averages alone do not explain why.

What remains unanswered

Whether one format improves chess skill more. These metadata contain no moves or evaluations, and the choice of format is not randomized. The original rated early/later analysis remains a separate question.
